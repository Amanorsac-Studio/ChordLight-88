| Audience | What they come for |
| --- | --- |
| **Worship and gospel keyboardists** | The number readout and sol-fa. They think in 1–4–5 and Do–Re–Mi, not in F♯m7♭5, and every other chord display speaks only letters. |

| **Producers and session players** | Naming a voicing they found by ear, mid-take, without stopping the DAW or reaching for a plugin. |
| **Content creators and streamers** | Three detachable always-on-top windows that sit over a screen recording and do not move when the chord changes. |

## 3. The problem it solves

Every chord-display tool on a Windows machine has the same flaw: it opens the MIDI
port for itself. The moment it runs, Kontakt goes quiet, or the DAW stops receiving,
or the app reports the device as busy — so the thing that was supposed to help you
play stops you playing.

Chordlight never opens a port exclusively. It listens alongside whatever else is
holding the controller. That is the whole reason it exists, and it is the first
thing to say in any piece of copy.

The second problem is language. Chord displays name chords in letters. A large part
of the keyboard-playing world — worship, gospel, choral, most of the teaching world
outside conservatoires — works in numbers and in sol-fa. Chordlight reads all three
at once, from the same hands.

## 7. What not to claim

Being straight about these keeps the reviews honest.

- **It is not a transcription or notation tool.** It names what is sounding now; it
  writes nothing down and exports nothing.
- **It does not analyse audio.** MIDI in, nothing else.
- **It is not a plugin.** No VST3, no AU — it is a standalone window. (A plugin
  version is a different product, not a roadmap promise to make here.)
- **Shared MIDI has one exception.** A controller whose manufacturer ships an
  exclusive-access driver cannot be shared by anything; route it through loopMIDI
  in that case. Say this in the FAQ rather than let a buyer discover it.
- **No cloud, so no sync.** Preferences live on the machine.

**How they were made.** Rendered from the shipping renderer
(`src/renderer/index.html`) at 2× with a stubbed MIDI input, so every pixel is the
real interface and the state is a real `Fmaj9`/`Am7`/`Cmaj7` played into it. Two
caveats before these go on a store page:

1. The capture environment has no Inter, JetBrains Mono or Barlow Condensed, so the
   text is set in the fallback stack. **Re-capture on a machine with the bundled
   fonts** — run `npm install` (which copies the fonts in) and shoot the built app —
   before these are used anywhere public. Layout and colour are exact; the
   letterforms are not.
2. The MIDI port reads `loopMIDI Port 1` because that is the stub's name. A capture
   from a real rig will read whatever is plugged in.
